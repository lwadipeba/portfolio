Brief Byte

An AI-powered briefing tool designed to help people stay informed without becoming overwhelmed by the volume of daily news and newsletters.

TeamSeven members
ToolsPython, Streamlit, Hugging Face
Output5–10 minute briefings
View project on GitHub ↗

The challenge

Useful information is often buried beneath volume.

Brief Byte brings multiple newsletter and RSS sources into one experience, identifies relevant content and produces a concise briefing based on the reader’s interests and available time.

The product was designed with traceability in mind. Users retain access to the original source links and the system includes an extractive fallback when a generative summary is unavailable.

How it works

From many sources to one useful briefing

1. Collect

Ingest articles from selected newsletters and RSS sources.

2. Rank

Use TF-IDF and cosine similarity to match content with reader interests.

3. Summarize

Use DistilBART with an extractive fallback to create a time-controlled briefing.

Determinants of Happiness

How strongly do economic resources, social support, personal freedom and other country conditions relate to happiness? This project compares a GDP-only baseline with a broader model of 137 countries.

Research scope137 countries
OutcomeLife evaluation, 0–10
MethodOLS with robust errors
Project repository ↗Access slides ↗Read paper ↗

Question and data

Happiness reflects more than income

The analysis asks how a country’s socioeconomic conditions relate to its residents’ life evaluations. The World Happiness Report measures those evaluations with the Cantril ladder: respondents place their life from 0, the worst possible life, to 10, the best possible life. I use three-year average scores from 2021–2023.

137country observations
0–10life-evaluation scale
2023World Happiness Report dataset
OLScountry-level regression

Economic and health

GDP per capita and healthy life expectancy.

Social and personal

Social support, freedom to make life choices and generosity.

Institutional and regional

Perceptions of corruption plus region indicators. Africa and the Middle East form the reference region.

The expanded model includes six measured predictors and regional controls. I use HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors.

Model comparison

The expanded model accounts for more variation

The GDP-only model explains 61.5% of the observed variation in country happiness scores. Adding the social, health, personal and institutional measures raises the in-sample R² to 84.6%, a 23.1 percentage-point increase.

R² summarizes fit in this sample; it does not show that the included conditions caused happiness. The expanded model’s adjusted R² is 83.5%.

Regression results

Social support and freedom remain strong positive signals

The expanded regression estimates each factor while holding the other included factors constant. My final regression shows positive associations for GDP per capita, social support, freedom and perceptions of corruption. Healthy life expectancy and generosity have positive estimates, but their intervals include zero.

95% interval above zero in the paper’s regression table95% interval crosses zero

Dots show coefficient estimates; horizontal lines show 95% confidence intervals from the paper’s final regression output. Predictor scales differ, so coefficient magnitudes are not a direct ranking of importance. For the corruption measure, the paper interprets higher values as perceptions of lower corruption.

Positive associations

GDP per capita, social support, freedom and corruption perceptions have intervals above zero after controlling for the other included variables.

Uncertain individual effects

Healthy life expectancy and generosity are not individually significant in the expanded model. I also tested health with GDP, and generosity with social support, together; each pair was jointly informative.

Regional controls

Region indicators are not statistically significant in the paper’s model, once the other factors are included.

Model check

Predicted scores track observed scores

I compare each country’s observed happiness score with the score predicted by my expanded model. Points close to the dashed 45-degree line are closer predictions; the remaining spread shows that meaningful differences are still left unexplained.

Interpretation and limits

Useful evidence, with important boundaries

The results support a broader view of national well-being. GDP matters in the analysis, but social support and freedom also carry independent positive associations. Because the study is observational, these estimates describe relationships across countries rather than the effect of a specific policy change.

The coefficients are country-level estimates. They cannot explain why one individual is happier than another, and country averages can hide differences within each country.

  • Life evaluations are self-reported and may reflect cultural differences in how people use the 0–10 scale.
  • The analysis includes 137 country observations and depends on the variables available in the 2023 dataset.
  • Related predictors can move together, making it difficult to isolate each factor’s contribution.
  • Other conditions, including inequality, education and employment, may also shape well-being.
Project takeaway: GDP alone leaves substantial variation unexplained. Social support, freedom and institutional perceptions add useful information, while the model’s fit should not be read as proof of causation.

Waymo vs Uber

After repeatedly using and loving Waymo, I wanted to look beyond the novelty and investigate whether autonomous ride-hailing could be a genuinely viable alternative to Uber.

ComparisonPrice and wait time
Safety records814 reports reviewed

GitHub ↗Read actual paper ↗

The question

Does a better ride experience translate into a viable service?

I compared Waymo and Uber across price, wait time, route performance and safety. The analysis shows a meaningful tradeoff: Waymo was less expensive on average, while Uber offered much shorter and more consistent wait times.

The project also examined incident reports carefully, separating the growth in total reports from injury severity and geographic concentration.

Discussion & conclusion

Waymo’s future depends on scale, trust and approval

What automation could change

I see Waymo’s promise in a different operating model. Removing the human driver could reduce some human-driver risks and support more consistent service as the system develops. Autonomous vehicles still require oversight, and automation does not make every trip risk-free.

What could slow adoption

Waymo’s expansion requires mapping new service areas, adapting and testing its system, securing local approvals and substantial investment. Public trust in autonomous vehicles remains another barrier.

I also treat the safety comparison cautiously: Waymo’s required NHTSA reports and Uber’s public safety reports cover different outcomes, so they do not support a direct incident-rate comparison.

My conclusion: Waymo is a credible potential competitor, but this localized comparison does not establish that it is ready to replace Uber or when it might reach comparable scale.

Pangaea

We designed Pangaea as a unified AI workspace for USC MSBA students. It brings academic, career and personal responsibilities into one daily plan, then helps each student decide what to handle first.

AudienceUSC MSBA students
Product conceptAI student workspace
Proposed pilotSix weeks
Open prototype ↗Project approach

The challenge

Student responsibilities arrive across separate systems.

Course deadlines may sit in an LMS or syllabus, recruiting messages arrive by email, and personal plans live somewhere else. Students move between these systems to piece together what needs attention and when.

Our team designed Pangaea to connect those signals into a useful daily plan. It considers the surrounding context so students can see what deserves attention now, what can come next and what can wait.

Every signal. One clear next step.

Directional research

Students reported difficulty deciding what to do next.

68%were unsure what to work on next several times a week
72%were dissatisfied with their current organization systems
80%wanted a prioritized list of academic deadlines
>80%checked numerous platforms to keep up with deadlines and events

These findings came from our USC MSBA student survey. The cohort was small, so we treat the results as directional evidence to guide the product and pilot, not as representative population estimates.

The product

One workspace brings four parts of student life together.

The prototype organizes information into connected areas. Shared context lets an academic deadline affect the timing of a recruiting follow-up or personal commitment.

Important Mail

Surfaces messages that need a response or action.

Academics

Collects courses, deadlines and workload.

Career

Organizes recruiting tasks and role opportunities.

Personal

Accounts for plans, errands and available time.

Prototype note: The live demo is a design preview with made-up data. It shows the intended experience, not live student records or validated product performance.

Context engine

Recommendations depend on the full picture.

Extract

Use only the sources a student approves, such as email, the LMS, calendar and syllabus documents.

Understand

Clean information and combine repeated deadlines into one task.

Prioritize

Weigh deadlines alongside workload, availability and career relevance.

Act

Show a recommended next step with context, plus what comes after it.

We designed the workflow so suggestions can be accepted, postponed or dismissed. Those choices would help us understand whether the recommendations fit students’ needs.

Proposed pilot

We planned a six-week test around student follow-through.

Primary measure

On-time completion, comparing Brightspace due dates with when students mark work complete.

Supporting measures

Time spent organizing, recommendation adoption, and the time between recruiting messages and follow-up.

Trust and wellbeing

Track privacy and permission safeguards, stress and alert fatigue alongside product outcomes.

Week 1

Baseline

Measure both groups before the pilot period.

Weeks 2–5

Pilot

The Pangaea group uses the product while both groups are tracked.

Week 6

Readout

Compare each group’s change against its own baseline.

Decision

Review

Consider the planned statistical threshold and guardrails before scaling.

This is an evaluation plan, not a report of proven impact. The proposed analysis uses intention-to-treat and difference-in-differences. The team planned to scale only if on-time completion improved at the pre-set significance threshold and no privacy or wellbeing guardrail was breached.

Project approach

From a student need to a testable product plan.

01 · Student needFragmented systems make it harder to see priorities across the week.
02 · ResearchOur MSBA survey offered directional evidence about planning and organization challenges.
03 · Product conceptA shared workspace connects mail, academics, career and personal responsibilities.
04 · Recommendation logicThe context engine cleans duplicate signals and ranks next actions using each student’s situation.
05 · EvaluationA six-week pilot would measure completion, organizing time, adoption and career follow-through.
06 · RoadmapStart with the prototype, test with an MSBA cohort, then consider trusted integrations and broader campus use.
Roadmap: We proposed moving from a personalized daily plan to an MSBA pilot, then to opt-in integrations with privacy controls, and later to possible use by programs and student support teams.

DealCheck

An AI brand-deal manager that helps independent influencers understand what they will earn, what they must deliver and what they might risk before accepting a partnership.

TeamLame Wadipeba, Helen Li, Leo Liu, Joshya Gupta
AudienceIndependent creators
OutputBrand Deal Health Report
Open prototype ↗

The opportunity

The headline fee hides the real cost.

Independent creators make business decisions using information scattered across contracts, email, calendars, financial records and social analytics. Production expenses, unlimited revisions, exclusivity, broad usage rights and payment delays can materially change a deal’s true value.

DealCheck connects this information and helps creators decide whether to accept, negotiate or decline an offer.

The business intelligence of a manager before creators can afford one.

Evidence

Creators face financial, capacity and audience pressure.

67%earned under $10,000 annually from content creation
48%reported receiving late payments
78%said burnout affects motivation
42%reported audience-brand tension

Sources represent separate CreatorIQ, Lumanu and Patreon/NewtonX populations and are not pooled.

Product framework

Three connected assessments

Financial Fit

Extracts compensation, expenses, payment timing and rights, then estimates effective hourly compensation.

Schedule Fit

Compares campaign obligations with available production capacity, deadlines and revision buffers.

Brand & Audience Fit

Checks alignment with the creator’s values, audience, competitor restrictions and creative freedom.

Brand Deal Health Report

Every report includes contract evidence, missing information, confidence indicators, prioritized negotiation points and an editable response.

Product rule: Missing critical information prevents a Green recommendation. A strong fee cannot outweigh an impossible deadline or prohibited brand category. Financial urgency remains separate from deal quality.

Proposed pilot

Test speed without sacrificing understanding.

≥80%informed reviews within ten minutes
≥50%median review-time reduction
≥95%critical-field extraction accuracy
≥90%recall of expert-labelled material risks
